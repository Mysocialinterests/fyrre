## The big question

You've surely heard it before: *"You are the master of your destiny,"* *"Create your own reality,"* *"Be authentic."* These phrases are everywhere — in motivational speeches, on social media, in companies, in advertising. They seem self-evident, as if they had always existed.

But that's not the case.

This obsession with the individual — with authenticity, autonomy, self-fulfillment — is a historically recent phenomenon. And it is profoundly transforming the way we live, work, love, and perceive ourselves.

This is exactly what a team of international researchers documents in the collective work *Société et subjectivité: transformations contemporaines* (2021), edited by Daniel Mercure and Marie-Pierre Bourdages-Sylvain, published by Presses de l'Université Laval.

## What is "subjectivity"? Let's be clear.

The word "subjectivity" can sound intimidating. In sociology, it simply refers to the way each individual **makes sense of the world** — how they interpret their life, build their identity, and give meaning to their actions and relationships.

It's not just "what you think of yourself." It's something deeper: the way society has shaped you to see yourself a certain way, to value certain things over others, to feel responsible for your happiness or guilty for your failures.

In other words: your subjectivity is the invisible filter through which you live your life. And that filter changes with the times.

## The great shift: individualization

Since the 1980s, our Western societies have been living through what sociologists call a vast process of **individualization**. The individual has become the central value of our societies — wanting to be the author of their own story, free in their choices, responsible for their successes as much as their failures.

This isn't an accident. It's the result of profound transformations: the rise of neoliberalism, the weakening of major collective institutions (the Church, unions, political parties), the transformation of work, and the explosion of communication technologies that let everyone "build an identity" online.

The result? An individual who wants to be unique, autonomous, authentic. An individual who manages their career like a personal brand, who picks their values à la carte, who rejects imposed identities.

But is this really liberation?

## What the researchers found

The collective work edited by Mercure and Bourdages-Sylvain brings together contributions from researchers across several countries — including well-known names in sociology such as Bernard Lahire, Jean-Claude Kaufmann, and Michel Wieviorka. Together, they examine how this individualization concretely manifests in different spheres of social life.

**Identity construction**: How do we build an identity today, when traditional reference points (religion, social class, origin) have lost their structuring power? The researchers show that self-construction has become a permanent — and often exhausting — project.

**Public and private space**: The boundary between public and private life is being redrawn. What was once strictly private (our opinions, our relationships, our emotions) is now exposed, performed, and publicly evaluated — especially on social media.

**New institutional dynamics**: Our relationship with institutions has changed. We no longer trust them automatically. We question them, work around them, personalize them. This creates both more individual freedom and more collective fragmentation.

**Forms of sociability and intimacy**: The ways we form bonds have also evolved. Relationships are more chosen, more negotiated, but also more fragile. Friendship, partnership, family — everything is being redefined through the lens of individual fulfillment.

## The dark side of autonomy

The researchers don't naively praise this individualization. They also expose its deep contradictions.

First tension: **freedom without a safety net**. When society values the individual as the sole party responsible for their life, it tends to erase the structural causes of inequality. Didn't succeed? That's your fault — not the system's. This logic weighs particularly heavily on those who start out with fewer resources.

Second tension: **identity fatigue**. Constantly building yourself, reinventing yourself, being authentic while meeting social expectations — this is invisible, constant work. Several researchers link this pressure to the rise of mental health issues in our societies.

Third tension: **the inequality of freedom**. Not everyone has the same access to this freedom to "build themselves." Subjectivity is also shaped by social class, gender, ethnic origin, and geography. The "free and autonomous" individual is often a privilege.

## Why this matters to us, here, now

In Quebec, these transformations are particularly visible. Quebec society lived through its own revolution — the Quiet Revolution — which accelerated the process of secularization and individualization. In a few decades, we went from a society tightly framed by collective institutions to one where the individual reigns.

The result? A creative, open, diverse society. But also a society where many people feel alone facing problems that are, in reality, collective. Mental health, burnout, loneliness, lack of meaning — these are problems that appear individual but are, in fact, social symptoms.

## What we take away

*Société et subjectivité* is not a self-help book. It is an X-ray of our era — rigorous, nuanced, necessary.

What the researchers are telling us, at bottom, is this: understanding how society shapes the way we see ourselves is the first step to no longer undergoing that shaping without questioning it.

Are we really the authors of our own lives? Perhaps. But the script was written well before we were born.
